Fine-Tuning Synonyms: Why Precise Language Matters

The words we use to describe our methods are not decoration. They are claims, and sloppy claims make results impossible to trust or reproduce.

The word that hides the method

In conversation and in papers, I keep seeing 'fine-tuning' used as a catch-all term for anything that happens after a pretrained model is loaded. Someone froze the backbone and trained a linear head: fine-tuning. Someone updated every weight for ten epochs: fine-tuning. Someone injected a handful of low-rank adapter matrices and trained only those: also, apparently, fine-tuning. These are different procedures with different compute costs, different failure modes, and different claims about what the model learned, yet the language collapses them into one word.

This matters because the term is doing real epistemic work. When I read that a model was 'fine-tuned' on a small dataset, I form an expectation about how much of the original pretrained representation survived, how much risk there is of catastrophic forgetting, and how comparable the result is to other reported numbers. If the actual method was prompt tuning with a few thousand trainable parameters, my expectations are wrong, and any comparison I draw against a fully fine-tuned baseline is unfair to both.

Precision is not pedantry here, it is part of the result. A method description is a specification. If I cannot tell from the text whether you updated 100 per cent or 0.1 per cent of the parameters, I cannot assess whether your reported gain is impressive or expected, and I certainly cannot reproduce it.

A worked example: three methods, one label

Suppose we have a pretrained encoder with 110 million parameters and we want to adapt it to a sentiment classification task with 20,000 labelled examples. Consider three plausible procedures, all of which someone might casually call fine-tuning.

  • Full fine-tuning: every one of the 110 million parameters is updated. Training needs a reasonably sized GPU, careful learning rate scheduling to avoid catastrophic forgetting, and typically a few epochs over the 20,000 examples.
  • Linear probing: the encoder is frozen entirely and only a new classification head, say 768 by 2 parameters, around 1,500 weights, is trained. This is sometimes called fine-tuning in casual speech, but it is a fundamentally different regime: the representation is fixed, and we are only learning a decision boundary on top of it.
  • Parameter-efficient tuning (for example low-rank adapters): perhaps 1 to 2 million additional parameters are inserted and trained while the original 110 million stay frozen. This sits between the other two in both compute and in how much the original representation is allowed to shift.

Now imagine three papers each report 91 per cent accuracy on the same test set and each describes the method as 'we fine-tuned the model on the task data'. The reader has no way of knowing which regime produced that number. If the full fine-tuning result used a learning rate of 2e-5 with a 500-step warmup, the 91 per cent might reflect fairly deep adaptation, including possibly memorising quirks of a small test set. If the linear probe achieved the same 91 per cent, that is arguably a more striking result, because it means the pretrained representation already separated the classes well, and almost no task-specific learning was needed. These are different scientific claims. Lumping them under one word erases the distinction that the reader most needs.

Why this bites hardest in evaluation

The place I notice this most is when comparing baselines. Suppose a paper claims a new parameter-efficient method matches full fine-tuning while using only 1 per cent of the trainable parameters. That is a genuinely useful claim, but only if the full fine-tuning baseline was actually full fine-tuning, with the same number of training steps, the same data split, and no extra hyperparameter search that the efficient method did not also receive. I have seen comparisons where the baseline description is just 'fine-tuned model', with no epoch count, no indication of whether early stopping was used, and no statement of how many parameters were frozen. Without that, the headline comparison is unfalsifiable: I cannot tell whether the efficient method is genuinely competitive or whether the baseline was simply under-trained.

Leakage-aware evaluation makes the same point from a different angle. If a dataset has near-duplicate examples across train and test splits, a fully fine-tuned model with 110 million adaptable parameters has far more capacity to memorise those duplicates than a linear probe with 1,500 parameters does. So the same leakage can produce wildly different inflation depending on which adaptation method was used, and a reader cannot judge that risk at all if the method is described only as 'fine-tuning'.

There is also a reproducibility cost that is easy to underestimate. If I try to replicate a result and guess wrong about which regime was used, I might freeze layers that should have been trainable, or train parameters that should have stayed fixed, and get a number nowhere near the reported one. I will then waste time debugging my own pipeline when the actual problem was an ambiguous method description in the source I was following.

What precise language actually looks like

Being precise does not require more jargon, it requires more specificity about things that are already simple to state. A good method description answers a short checklist: which parameters were updated and roughly how many, what the frozen components were, how many training examples and epochs were used, and whether any part of the pretrained model was reinitialised. None of that requires new vocabulary, it just requires writing down facts instead of reaching for a single convenient verb.

I try to hold myself to naming the regime explicitly: full fine-tuning, linear probing, or parameter-efficient tuning, and stating the trainable parameter count alongside the total. It takes one extra sentence and it removes an entire category of ambiguity for the reader. The practical habit worth adopting is simple: before you write 'fine-tuned', ask whether a reader could reproduce your exact setup from that word alone. If the answer is no, say what you actually did instead.
