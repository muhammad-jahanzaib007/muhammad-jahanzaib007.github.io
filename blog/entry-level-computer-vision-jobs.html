Computer Vision Jobs Decoded: What 'Entry Level' Really Means

A guide to reading between the lines of computer vision job adverts, so you stop disqualifying yourself from roles you are actually ready for.

The confusing label

If you have spent any time scrolling computer vision job boards, you will have noticed a strange pattern. A posting says 'entry level', then lists three years of production experience, familiarity with deploying models on edge devices, and a working knowledge of at least two deep learning frameworks. This is not a typo or a cruel joke. It reflects how the term has drifted from its plain-English meaning into something closer to industry shorthand, and understanding that drift is the single most useful thing you can do before applying.

I think this matters because misreading the label causes two opposite failures. Some candidates with genuinely strong fundamentals see the experience requirements and talk themselves out of applying, assuming the posting is only for seasoned engineers. Others apply to everything labelled 'entry level' without checking whether the role actually suits someone building their first portfolio, and burn time on applications that were never a realistic match. Neither group is reading the posting for what it is actually telling them.

'Entry level' in this context usually means entry level for the company's internal ladder, not entry level for the field as a whole. It signals that the role sits below senior and staff positions, that you will not be expected to design architecture decisions unsupervised, and that there is a mentor or lead who reviews your work. It does not mean the company expects you to learn convolutional networks on the job. The technical floor is usually set earlier than that, somewhere in your degree, bootcamp, or self-study.

Decoding the requirements list

Take a realistic example. A posting asks for 'experience with object detection pipelines, familiarity with PyTorch or TensorFlow, understanding of evaluation metrics such as mAP and IoU, and exposure to deployment on constrained hardware'. Read literally, this sounds like a job for someone who has already shipped a product. Read as a filter, it is telling you what concepts you need to be conversant in during an interview, not what you need years of paid experience doing.

Here is the distinction that matters: there is a difference between having built something and having shipped something at scale. If you trained a detector on a public dataset, split your data properly, computed mAP at a few IoU thresholds, and can explain why your precision-recall curve looks the way it does, you satisfy the spirit of 'familiarity with evaluation metrics' even if you have never touched a production codebase. A hiring manager asking about IoU in an interview wants to know you understand what the metric penalises, not whether you have a specific job title on your CV.

Deployment requirements are usually the most intimidating line item, and also the most negotiable. A posting might mention 'deployment on constrained hardware' because the team eventually ships to embedded devices, but for an entry level hire this often just means you understand why model size and latency matter, not that you have personally optimised a quantised model for a specific chip. If your project work shows you thought about inference time at all, even informally, that is often enough to clear the bar in a screening conversation.

The years-of-experience number is the trickiest part to decode, because it is frequently inherited from a template rather than calibrated to the actual role. A posting asking for '2 plus years' alongside the word 'entry level' is common enough that many recruiters privately acknowledge it as inconsistent copy, carried over from a generic job description template. This does not mean the number is meaningless, but it does mean you should weigh it against the rest of the posting rather than treating it as a hard gate.

What actually gets you through the door

Since the written requirements are an imperfect proxy, it helps to think about what they are a proxy for. Teams hiring for entry level computer vision roles are usually trying to answer three questions: can this person reason about a vision problem correctly, can they write code that other people can read and extend, and can they be trusted not to quietly ship a broken evaluation.

That last point is worth dwelling on, because it is where many self-taught candidates actually have an advantage over the requirements list. If you can explain, with a concrete example, why evaluating a detector on images that share scenes or lighting conditions with your training set gives an optimistic and misleading score, you are demonstrating exactly the kind of judgement that separates someone who ran a tutorial from someone who understands the task. A portfolio project that documents a careful train, validation, and test split, with a short note on why you avoided leakage between near-duplicate frames from the same video, communicates more than a line claiming '2 years experience' ever could.

Practically, this means your application materials should translate your project work into the vocabulary the posting uses, rather than assuming the reader will make the connection themselves. If a job description mentions 'robustness to distribution shift', and your project tested a model trained on daytime images against a small set of dusk or overcast images, say so explicitly in those terms. You are not inflating your experience, you are making the match between what you did and what they asked for legible to someone skimming forty applications.

It also means you should treat the requirements list as a conversation starter rather than a locked gate. If you meet most of the core technical asks and are missing only the years-of-experience line or one specific tool, apply anyway and let the interview process do its job. Companies that genuinely intend a role for early-career candidates expect some gap between the wish list and the person they hire. The honest approach is not to pretend you have experience you do not, but to be clear and specific about what you have actually done, and let that stand on its own merits.
