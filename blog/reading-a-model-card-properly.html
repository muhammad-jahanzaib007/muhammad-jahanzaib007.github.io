Reading a Model Card Properly: What It Hides

Model cards promise transparency, but a careful reading often reveals more by what is absent than what is stated. Here is how to read one like a reviewer rather than a consumer.

Why the document exists and what it is not

A model card is meant to answer a simple question: can I trust this model for my use case? It usually lists the architecture, the training data at a high level, some benchmark scores, intended uses, and a limitations section. For anyone doing serious evaluation work, model cards are a welcome improvement over the alternative, which is nothing at all. But treating a model card as a complete specification is a mistake I think a lot of practitioners make, often because the document is formatted like a spec sheet, with neat tables and percentages that invite you to stop thinking critically the moment you see a number.

The honest way to read a model card is to treat it as a claim made by the people with the most incentive to present the model favourably. That is not an accusation of dishonesty; it is just how incentives work. A team that spent months training a model will naturally report the benchmarks that flatter it and describe limitations in the mildest language that is still defensible. Reading properly means asking, for every sentence, what would have to be true for this to be misleading without being false.

Consider a card that states the model achieves 91 percent accuracy on a named benchmark. That number is probably accurate as measured. What it does not tell you is whether that benchmark's test set shares any examples, templates, or near-duplicates with the training data, whether the 91 percent is an average across subpopulations that hides a 65 percent score on a minority group, or whether the benchmark itself is already considered close to saturated by the research community. None of those omissions require the card to lie. They just require it to report less than the full picture, which is the default behaviour of any summary document.

What the benchmark numbers quietly leave out

Take a concrete example. Suppose a card reports that a text classification model scores 88 percent F1 on a held-out test set drawn from news articles. That single number compresses an enormous amount of decision-making into something that looks objective. Was the test set collected in the same time period as training data, or six months later? If the articles were scraped from the same sources, topic drift between training and test is unlikely to be severe, but if the test set is older, the model may be quietly benefiting from topics and phrasing it has effectively memorised rather than generalised to.

Class balance is another quiet omission. If the test set is 85 percent one class and 15 percent another, an 88 percent F1 score could be achieved by a model that is only moderately good at the minority class but excellent at the majority one, especially if F1 is reported as a single aggregate rather than per class. I have seen cases where a headline metric looks strong purely because the evaluation set mirrors the training distribution's imbalance, which tells you almost nothing about performance in a deployment setting where the balance is different.

Then there is the question of how many runs produced that number. A single training run with a lucky seed can land a few points higher than the average of five runs with different seeds. A model card that reports one number without a standard deviation or a range is not necessarily cheating, but it is withholding information that would let you judge how stable that number actually is. If the true performance fluctuates between 83 and 89 percent depending on the random seed, the headline figure of 88 percent is a single sample, not a reliable estimate, and treating it as a fixed property of the model is a subtle but consequential error.

The limitations section is often the least examined part

Limitations sections tend to follow a predictable template: the model may underperform on out-of-distribution inputs, may reflect biases present in the training data, and should not be used for high-stakes decisions without human oversight. These statements are true of almost every model ever built, which is exactly the problem. They are vague enough to be unfalsifiable and specific enough to look responsible. A limitations section that says this does little to help someone decide whether the model is suitable for a particular task, because it does not say which out-of-distribution inputs, which biases, or which stakes.

What a genuinely useful limitations section would include is harder to produce and rarer to see: specific subpopulations where performance has been measured and found weaker, specific input lengths or formats that cause degraded behaviour, and an honest account of what was not tested at all. The absence of a stated limitation is not evidence of absence of that limitation; it is often evidence that nobody measured it. A card that is silent on performance across different languages, dialects, or demographic groups has not told you the model is fine on those axes. It has told you that axis was not part of the evaluation plan.

Training data descriptions suffer from a similar gap. Cards frequently describe data sources at a category level, such as web text, books, and licensed datasets, without disclosing proportions, collection dates, filtering criteria, or deduplication procedures. This matters enormously for leakage. If a card does not specify how overlap between training and evaluation data was checked, you should assume it was checked loosely or not at all, because teams that did rigorous leakage audits tend to mention it, since it reflects well on them. The absence of a claim about something that would be flattering to claim is itself informative.

A practical way to read any card

My approach is to read a model card the way a careful reviewer reads a paper's limitations section: looking for what is missing rather than what is stated. For every benchmark number, I ask about the test set's independence from training data, the class balance, and whether multiple runs are reported. For every limitation statement, I ask whether it names a specific, testable condition or just gestures at a general category of risk. For the training data description, I ask whether proportions and collection windows are given, and whether leakage checking is mentioned at all.

None of this means model cards are useless; they are a genuine step forward from the days when models shipped with no documentation whatsoever, and a well-written card can save hours of exploratory testing. But a model card should be the start of due diligence, not the end of it. If a deployment decision matters, the card tells you what to go and verify yourself: run the model on a held-out sample from your own distribution, check performance across the subgroups that matter to you, and treat any number without a reported variance as a point estimate with unknown uncertainty. The quiet omissions are not usually malicious; they are the natural consequence of summarising something complicated into a document meant to be read in five minutes. Reading it properly just means remembering that the five-minute version was never going to be the whole story.
